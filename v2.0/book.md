### Chapter 2 — The Night-Blooming

The scent arrived before the light did.

Amara woke at four in the morning to something sweet and insistent pressing through the open window, a fragrance so thick it felt like a hand laid on her face. She lay still in the dark, her heart doing its slow arithmetic of loss, and tried to name it. Not the frangipani from Mrs. Odongo's yard below. Not the orange blossom that drifted up from the avenue in March. This was heavier, closer, almost personal — the smell of a thing that had decided to announce itself.

She sat up. The ceiling fan clicked its familiar complaint. The apartment was exactly as she had left it — Daniel's chair by the window, his slippers still angled under it as if he'd stepped out of them a moment ago and would step back in any second. The dust on the television. The photograph of them on the sideboard, both of them younger, laughing at something no one could remember.

The scent came from the balcony.

She almost didn't go. There was a discipline to her grief, a system of small avoidances. The balcony door had been shut since the funeral, because the balcony was where Daniel had kept his plants, and his plants had kept dying, and she had not had the strength to bury anything else. The jasmine in the cracked blue pot had been the last to go. She had watched it turn from green to yellow to a brittle brown, and she had thought, *Good. That's finished. That's all finished.*

But the smell — the smell was jasmine.

Amara pushed back the mosquito net and walked to the balcony door. Her bare feet on the cool concrete. The latch sticking the way it always stuck. She had to use both hands, and that small struggle felt like something Daniel would have laughed at — *you always fight with that door, woman* — and then the door gave way, and the night air hit her, and she saw.

The jasmine was covered in flowers.

Every branch. Every stem. The dead brown sticks she had not watered in four months were hidden under a cascade of white blossoms, each one no bigger than her thumbnail, each one open to the dark sky and breathing out that impossible perfume. The blooms moved slightly, though there was no wind. They moved like a crowd shifting its weight, like a room full of people turning to look at one person.

Amara stood in the doorway.

"Daniel," she said.

It was not a question. She did not know why she said it.

The leaves rustled.

And that was when she heard him.

His voice came out of the foliage the way a song comes out of a radio you thought was broken — faint at first, then clear, then unmistakable. It was not coming from his mouth. There was no mouth. It was coming from the green itself, from the way the leaves arranged themselves around the sound, the way the petals caught and held the vibration.

*Amara.*

She gripped the doorframe.

*You let the kettle boil dry again.*

It was the most ordinary sentence in the world, and it broke her open. That was the thing he always said to her, in the mornings, when she got distracted by the birds on the sill and forgot the water was on. He would come in from the balcony, wipe his hands on his trousers, and say it with that particular tenderness, as if the burned kettle were a small miracle she had performed.

"Daniel." Her voice came out cracked. "You're dead."

The leaves did not argue. They did not say they were sorry. They rustled again, and the voice came back, softer now, the way he used to speak to her when she was sad and pretending not to be.

*I know. I'm still here.*

Amara moved forward without deciding to and sank onto the low concrete wall of the balcony, her knees giving way. The jasmine was at eye level. She could see the individual flowers now, each one perfect, each one newly made, stamens dusted with gold. There was dew on the petals, though it was the dry season, and the dew was warm, and it smelled like him — the particular smell of Daniel after he had been working in the sun, sweat and soap and something indefinably his.

She reached out and touched a flower.

It was real. It was cool and waxy and real, and it opened a little further under her finger, the way a sleeping animal might lean into a familiar hand.

"What are you?" she whispered.

The leaves made a sound like a chuckle. She would have sworn it.

*Your husband.* A pause. *Or a reasonable facsimile.*

She laughed. It came out as a sob, but it was a laugh. That was the kind of thing he would say — *reasonable facsimile* — the old-fashioned words he liked, the ones he used to deploy with a grin when the television weatherman was wrong. She had forgotten that. She had forgotten so much, and the forgetting had felt like betrayal, and here was a plant reminding her.

Below, in the courtyard, a light went on.

Amara looked down. The courtyard of the apartment block was a square of cracked concrete and hopeful green, a place where everyone kept something growing — pots of basil on windowsills, a papaya tree that had seeded itself by the drainage grate, the bougainvillea that Mrs. Odongo had trained up the wall until it looked like a waterfall of magenta. Amara had always thought of the courtyard as the building's shared living room, the place where the laundry lines crisscrossed and the children rode their bicycles in circles and the old men played draft under the mango tree.

But now, in the pre-dawn dark, the courtyard was moving.

She saw the papaya tree shake its leaves as if someone were climbing it. She saw the bougainvillea pulse, a wave of color running through its blossoms, and she heard — she could not help but hear — a chorus of small voices rising up from the earth, from the pots, from the vines, all of them murmuring at once, all of them speaking in the tones of the dead.

*...sisi, the tomatoes need staking...*

*...did you lock the door, Edith, did you lock it...*

*...I never told him, I never told him I was proud...*

Amara's breath caught.

She had known, of course. The building was old, and the dead had lived here in numbers. Mr. Kato in 2B, who had grown the most beautiful roses on the city block and died of a heart attack among them, found by the postman with soil still on his hands. Edith in 3C, the old woman who had kept a herb garden in old cooking-oil tins and made tea for everyone who was sick. The young man in 1A — Amara had never known his name — who had planted an avocado pit in a jam jar as a school project and then grown up and grown old and died with the avocado tree now taller than the building, its roots cracking the foundation of the courtyard wall.

Their plants had stayed. That was the thing about the dead, she thought. They left their green behind.

Now the green was speaking.

She turned back to the jasmine. In the gray light of not-quite-morning, the flowers seemed to glow from within, and the voice from the leaves was patient, waiting.

"Daniel," she said again, and this time the word was a prayer. "How long will you stay?"

*As long as the plant lives.*

"And the plant —" She looked at the dry soil, at the cracks in the blue pot, at the miracle that had no business existing. "How long will it live?"

The leaves went quiet for a long moment. Somewhere in the courtyard, a voice was laughing — a low, rich laugh that sounded like the young man who had grown the avocado tree. The sound moved up through the dark and mingled with the jasmine's scent.

*Until you water it, Amara.*

She understood.

She stood up, her knees aching, her heart a strange and tender instrument she had thought broken beyond tuning. She walked back into the apartment, through the living room with Daniel's chair and Daniel's slippers and the photograph of them laughing, and into the kitchen. She filled the watering can — the green one he had bought at the market, the one with the dent in the side, the one she had shoved to the back of the cupboard. She filled it at the sink. The water was cold. It smelled of iron and the city.

She carried it back to the balcony. The jasmine was waiting, still blooming, still breathing out its impossible perfume. The leaves rustled as she approached, and she could have sworn they leaned toward her, the way Daniel used to lean toward her when she entered a room, his whole body turning like a plant toward light.

She poured the water slowly, carefully, around the base of the stem. The soil drank it greedily. The flowers trembled.

*Thank you*, said the leaves.

Amara set the watering can down. She sat on the low wall again, drawing her knees up, wrapping her arms around them. The first bird of the morning cleared its throat somewhere in the mango tree. The sky was going from black to blue. The jasmine was still, now, as if listening.

"Tell me about the day we met," she said.

The leaves rustled. A single petal fell, drifting down to rest on her bare foot, light as a kiss.

And the voice that came out of the green was warm, and patient, and full of the ordinary miracle of a life shared, and it began to tell her.

---

The sun came up the way it always did, slanted and golden, finding the cracks in the world. Amara did not move. She sat on the cold concrete wall while the light moved across the courtyard, while the voices below grew louder and then, one by one, fell into a listening hush. She heard Mrs. Odongo begin to weep — a sound of pure, astonished joy — and then to laugh, and then to answer something in a low, shaking voice.

Amara rose on stiff legs and went to the railing.

Mrs. Odongo was in the courtyard below, kneeling in front of her bougainvillea, her white nightdress pooled around her on the concrete. The bougainvillea had never looked like this. Every branch was thick with magenta blossoms, more than the plant had ever produced, more than seemed physically possible, and the old woman was talking to it as if it were her husband.

"You left your glasses on the television," she was saying. "I put them in the drawer. With your letters. I kept your letters."

The bougainvillea rustled, and a voice came from it — a man's voice, gruff and gentle — and Mrs. Odongo bowed her head and pressed her forehead to the blossoms.

Amara watched. Her own grief, which had been a locked room, was suddenly a door standing open.

She went back inside, put on her sandals, and walked down the two flights of stairs to the courtyard. The building was coming alive — lights flicking on, doors opening, neighbors stepping out in dressing gowns and slippers, drawn by the smell, by the sound, by the impossible green. Mr. Tumusiime from 4A was standing under the avocado tree with his hand on the bark, his mouth open. The young mother from 2C was holding her baby and crying silently while the mint on her windowsill whispered something in a voice Amara could not quite catch.

The courtyard was full of small gods, and they were all speaking.

Amara walked to Mrs. Odongo and knelt beside her. The old woman looked up, her face wet, her eyes wide and young.

"You hear him too?" Mrs. Odongo said.

"Yes," said Amara. "I hear him."

"My Samuel. He's in the bougainvillea. He says he's sorry for the time he forgot my birthday. He says he never forgot again. He says he's proud of me for keeping the plant alive." She laughed, a sound cracked down the middle. "I nearly killed it. Three times. The caterpillars got it once."

*But you didn't let it die*, said the bougainvillea, in its gruff, gentle voice. *You never let me die.*

Mrs. Odongo clutched Amara's hand. Her fingers were cold. "Is this real? Am I dreaming?"

Amara thought of the jasmine. She thought of Daniel saying *you let the kettle boil dry again*, and how it had sounded exactly like him, how it had carried the whole weight of their life together in eight plain words.

"It's real," she said. "I don't know how, but it's real."

They knelt together in the courtyard, two widows among the flowering dead, while the sun climbed higher and the voices of the leaves grew quiet and contented, like people who had said what needed saying and were satisfied to simply be heard.

When Amara finally went back upstairs, the jasmine was still blooming. She watered it again, though the soil was still damp, because it seemed like the thing to do. The leaves rustled their thanks. She sat in Daniel's chair — for the first time since he died — and looked at the photograph on the sideboard.

"You're really here," she said. "In the leaves."

*In the leaves*, agreed the jasmine. *And in the roots. And in the soil. I'm in everything you water, Amara.*

She closed her eyes. The perfume filled the apartment. The kettle sat cold and silent on the stove, and she thought — for the first time in four months — that she might make tea.
